Math

Three tiers: explicit inline LaTeX, explicit display LaTeX, and defensive auto-detect for bare unicode math glyphs.

.math-inline $f(x) = x^2 + 1$ Wrapped in $...$ by the agent; rendered via KaTeX. Math font fallback chain + italic; sub/sup are upright per math convention.

The function f(x) = x2 + 1 is differentiable everywhere on ℝ, with derivative f′(x) = 2x. At x = 3 the slope is 6 — a clean numeric anchor for the rate of change at that point.

.math-display $$\frac{dV}{dt} = 4πr^2 · \frac{dr}{dt}$$ Block-level, centered, larger. Set off from prose with vertical breathing room. Used when the equation is itself the point.

For a sphere of radius r whose radius grows over time, the rate of volume change is

dV/dt = 4πr2 · dr/dt

which is the chain rule unpacked — the rate of V with respect to r, times the rate of r with respect to t.

.math-glyph auto-wrapped per char The bare-unicode-glyph auto-detect pass. Each loose math char in prose (composition, partial, integral, sum, etc.) gets a quiet typographic shift. No parser; just a unicode codepoint table.

The composition ∘ is associative but not commutative: (f ∘ g) ∘ h = f ∘ (g ∘ h), but in general f ∘ g ≠ g ∘ f. Similarly the partial derivative ∂, the integral ∫, the sum ∑, and the gradient ∇ all carry a slight typographic shift so the eye knows they're symbols, not punctuation.

.variable x, dV/dt, f(x) Distinct from prose body italic. Used for ad-hoc variable references in flowing text where wrapping in full LaTeX would be heavy.

Let x be the input. Define f(x) as the function we care about, and g(x) as the inner function. The composite f(g(x)) takes x through g first, then through f.

Define a typed wrapper around the chain-rule application:

function chainRule(f, g, x) {
  // (f ∘ g)'(x) = f'(g(x)) · g'(x)
  const inner = g(x);
  const outer = derivative(f, inner);
  const innerRate = derivative(g, x);
  return outer * innerRate;
}

Citations & passages

Inline citation chips for sourced facts; passage blocks for quoted material with attribution.

The chain rule for composite functions states that the derivative of a composition is the product of the outer derivative at the inner value and the inner derivative Stewart §3.5. This generalizes cleanly to three or more nested functions Stewart §3.6; the proof via the limit definition Apostol Vol. 1 Ch. 4 is the cleanest among the standard treatments.

.passage <blockquote class="passage"> Set-off italic block for direct quotation from source material. Source attribution as mono kicker beneath. Editorial register; uses display font.

From the textbook's introduction to the chain rule:

The chain rule is one of the most useful and most often misapplied rules of differentiation. The misapplication usually comes from forgetting that the derivative of the outer function must be evaluated at the inner function, not at the original variable. Stewart, Calculus 8e · §3.5 · p. 197

Definitions & concepts

Three registers: first-introduction definition; concept reference (link to concept map); glossary term (hover for definition).

.definition first introduction Used the FIRST time a term is introduced in a session. Semibold + accent underline. Subsequent mentions revert to plain prose; the underline is the "this was just defined" marker.

A derivative of a function at a point is the instantaneous rate of change of the function's value with respect to its input. A composite function is a function built by applying one function to the output of another. The chain rule is the differentiation rule for composite functions: it relates the composite's derivative to the derivatives of its component functions.

.concept-ref <a class="concept-ref"> Link to a concept-map node. Italic + dashed underline + § sigil. Clicking opens the concept in the concept map panel.

The chain rule sits at the intersection of three concepts you've already worked through: composite functions, derivatives as rates, and function composition. Linking to those nodes helps the tutor know what context to assume.

.glossary <abbr class="glossary"> Dotted underline; cursor: help. Hover (mocked here as static) reveals a definition popover. Used for jargon the student should know but might want to refresh on.

A function is differentiable on an interval if its derivative exists at every point in that interval. We say a function is continuous if small input changes produce small output changes — every differentiable function is continuous, but the reverse isn't true.

Callouts

Four registers: theorem (primary assertion), lemma (supporting claim), hint (friendly nudge), warning (common pitfall).

.callout--theorem boxed primary assertion Named, full-weight border, dark icon. The big claims — theorems, propositions, formal statements. Title is display italic; body is body text.
Theorem
Chain rule for differentiable functionsIf g is differentiable at x and f is differentiable at g(x), then the composite f ∘ g is differentiable at x, and (f ∘ g)′(x) = f′(g(x)) · g′(x).
.callout--lemma secondary support Subdued background, thinner border. Used for supporting claims, intermediate results, technical lemmas — anything supporting a bigger statement.
Lemma
Continuity from differentiabilityIf f is differentiable at a point a, then f is continuous at a. This is the standard precondition we use to ensure the chain rule's hypotheses are met.
.callout--hint amber tint, friendly Friendly nudge or suggestion. Amber tint pulled from --color-warning at 8% mix with surface. Used when the tutor offers a way to think about something without it being a hard correction.
Hint
Picture the balloonWhenever the chain rule formula feels abstract, picture a balloon inflating: the radius grows at some rate, and the volume grows at the radius rate times how much the volume changes per unit radius. Two rates, one product.
.callout--warning red tint, pitfall Common mistake or anti-pattern. Red tint from --color-danger at 8%. Used when the tutor wants to flag something students typically get wrong.
Pitfall
Forgetting to evaluate at the inner valueThe most common chain-rule mistake is writing (f ∘ g)′(x) = f′(x) · g′(x). The outer derivative must be evaluated at g(x), not at x — otherwise you're computing the wrong derivative entirely.

fig. 1 · Chain rule applied to a balloon

A spherical balloon's radius grows at 2 cm/s when the radius is 5 cm. Find the rate of volume change at that moment.

The volume formula is V = (4/3)πr3. Differentiating with respect to r gives dV/dr = 4πr2. Applying the chain rule:

dV/dt = 4πr2 · dr/dt = 4π(25)(2) = 200π ≈ 628.3 cm3/s
✓ verified · sympy.diff(V.subs(r, r_t), t)

Procedural steps

Numbered step lists for sequential procedures, algorithms, proof outlines. Monospace step counter; body text per step.

.procedure <ol class="procedure"> Auto-numbered via CSS counter. Each step is a hairline-separated row with a mono number on the left and body content on the right. Replaces ad-hoc <ol> when the steps are substantive and benefit from visual separation.

Applying the chain rule in four steps:

  1. Identify the outer and inner functions. For y = sin(x2): outer is sin(u), inner is u = x2.
  2. Differentiate the outer with respect to its own argument: d/du [sin(u)] = cos(u).
  3. Differentiate the inner with respect to x: du/dx = 2x.
  4. Multiply, evaluating the outer derivative at the inner function: dy/dx = cos(x2) · 2x = 2x · cos(x2).
